Menu Design Lessons From Omakase: What Every Restaurateur Can Borrow

Omakase means “I’ll leave it up to you.” For a chef, it’s the ultimate expression of confidence. For a guest, it’s an act of trust. At Wakuda in Las Vegas, where the private eight-seat omakase room sits alongside a full restaurant and a secret bar, we see how much every operator can learn from that relationship.

Let seasonality lead

A menu built around what’s best today — not what was best when the menu was printed — gives guests a reason to come back. Even restaurants with fixed menus can build in a rotating feature, a seasonal special or a chef’s selection that keeps the kitchen sharp and the offer fresh.

“Think about the rhythm of the meal, not just the list of dishes.”

Control the pace

Omakase is paced course by course, so the chef decides when each bite lands. Every restaurant can borrow the idea: think about the rhythm of the meal, not just the list of dishes. Where do guests slow down? Where should there be a surprise?

Make fewer choices better

A shorter, more curated menu reduces ticket times, waste and decision fatigue — and signals confidence. If you can’t say why a dish is on the menu, it probably shouldn’t be.

Design the room for the experience

Wakuda’s space, inspired by Tokyo’s Shinjuku ward, includes a Kumiko-style ceiling, a terrace with views of Las Vegas Boulevard and a hidden bar. Each space supports a different kind of evening. The lesson: match the environment to the moment you’re trying to create.
